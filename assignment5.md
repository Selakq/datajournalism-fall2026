Link to article https://projects.propublica.org/black-snow/

Title: The Smoke Comes Every Year. Sugar Companies Say the Air is Safe

Lede:This article has an anecdotal lead about the Sonson brothers who live in an area near whose air has been impacted by the pollution from sugar companies that have caused respiratory issues, hospitalizing them for 8 weeks. The lede, just one sentence, is impactful in itself, but still leaves the reader with questions and wanting to know more. Without reading the rest of the article, the reader does not know what is causing these respiratory issues, where the brothers live, or really who they are. As the story continues, the reader learns in the second graf the brothers are just 5 and 6 years old, and have been having health issues since they were toddlers. The nutgraf then tells the reader about how the brothers, their family, and the entire area have a whole process to monitor the air quality in their area. 
I think the use of an anecdotal lede for this story was the right choice because it tugs at the readers heartstrings, but also makes the story more interesting and understandable than maybe a hard news lede would. Even though the lede itself is just one sentence, it still tells a story and the reader knows the article will be about health impacts. However, aside from the title the sugar factory or location are not mentioned until 4 grafs into the article after the lede. I think this makes the story take a little while to get to the point, especially since some details would have worked better in the second graf or nutgraf.
The data: The data in this article was about how the smoke and pollution from sugar factories has impacted the health and environment of the populated surrounding areas over an 18-month span. The data found the government's monitoring of the area was inadequate, and potentially dangerous to the communities. ProPublica found through their analysis that Pollution spikes frequently reached approximately four times the area's average pollution levels, specifically between 9 a.m. and 8 p.m., when sugar cane burning typically takes place. Experts interviewed by Propublica believe there is a strong correlation between the sugar burning and the air quality issues. Two pm2.5 air quality monitors were used.
One question I have is when does the line get drawn between a data visual and a design visual? Some maps show specific areas with pm 2.5 air monitors, where the sugar factories are, and where smoke is detected. However, the ones that are just using color and outlines to show area seem less data and more design focused to me? 
A critique I have is that two sensors seems like a small scope for the data and point the authors are trying to collect. 

Sources: Human sources are at the heart of this article because from the lead to the end, the journalists use lived experiences from the people impacted from the sugar companies, both on a health and environmental level, to show the impacts sugar companies have had on the surrounding areas. The stories from human experiences also served as a segue into the larger issue. I think human stories made the story, and without them (if the story was solely stats, experts and data), the story would be less engaging and missing the most important part: people are affected. 
For example: the lede being about two toddlers who were in the hospital for 8 weeks because of asthma they have struggled with since basically birth makes the reader sympathize with them. 
From the expert perspective, the authors quoted Ricardo Cisneros, an environmental health professor and expert on air quality monitoring that called out the discrepancies reported by the Belle Glade minotaur as concerning, showing the seriousness of the issue and the need for further examination and future change. 
“You have data now saying you have an instrument that is not working properly,” said Cisneros, who reviewed state documents describing the monitor’s problems at the request of The Post and ProPublica. “I would question its validity.”
The combination of both experts on air quality, the people impacted, the nurses caring for people made for a really meaningful holistic approach. I also appreciated the citing of certain laws that explain why the sugar companies were able to continue their work causing the heavy pollution. I also loved that they included the perspective of people who work for the sugar companies because they need a job. I can't think of how they could have sourced any better. 
Story structure:: This article used a combination of a narrative structure, inverted pyramid and martini glass. The anecdotal lede being the start and story of the first 5 or so grafs immediately makes this a story-telling and human interest story. I believe there are some aspects of an inverted pyramid as well because the story is structured to highlight the human aspect while also bringing in the hard news, data, etc as it becomes more important and central while not worrying too much about chronological order. And, the martini glass structure comes from that same idea of it starting from a small story and expanding into a wider theme about health and environmental issues.

Numbers: I do not think the numbers in the story made the article confusing, and I believe that the writers intended for readers to not be numbers experts or understand pm 2.5 completely, but to have general ideas from the visuals. 
Pm 2.5 is explained which served as base knowledge for understanding a lot of the graphs. There were also a lot of legends/keys for the maps showing different colors and even shapes to mark different areas and what they mean for the map data visuals.
Numbers in this story were also used to show distance smoke travels, and the time it takes to know the air is safe. However some charts do not have clearly labeled axes. While the reader could guess from context, the labels would have been nice to have. 

Data viz: This story used the “scrolly” method. It starts with a map where a box zooms in on Florida, a certain area in Florida, and outlines the counties impacted by sugar companies. Aside from a map, this story had over 10 visuals including data visuals, multimedia, and other designs to tell the story visually. Maps are used to show distance between sensors and the smoke levels by area, and the graphs show the numerical changes in smoke levels. The maps do not have exact numerical data for the distance, it is more the use of color to show how much smoke there is. It is a helpful visual I think was good to be included, but it certainly needed the line charts to make sense. 
There were a lot of different visualizations, graphics, and multimedia used in this article. Almost all of the data visuals were interactive, which was helpful because all of the types of data viz were timeline based, so the reader can click different areas of each visual to see different data at a different point in time about smoke levels or air quality. 
Some of the best and most important visuals were the line charts that, as the reader scrolled, line charts showing different dates and different peaks in pm2.5 levels changing throughout the 18-month span. As the reader keeps scrolling, these charts combine into two large multi-line charts showing the difference of when smoke is blown towards the area versus away from it. It is accompanied by two map visuals that use color to show what the molded smoke levels look like on a map and the distance from the sensors.  
I think the use of multiple different data visuals, including repeating graphics, was a good choice. Different visuals helped the reader understand different concepts, and almost all of them were interactive, allowing the reader to see the specific changes over time and when they happened. I wish some of them were more clearly labeled with x and y axis titles.
A critique I have is that some of the data could be potentially misleading. For example, the smoke plum maps do not clearly distinguish that it is a  projection and not a direct measurement of smoke exposure. The maps also do not show demographic context or the actual distance between where humans are versus the sugar companies. 

Methods: This article has a whole methodology section and the nerd box completely strengthened the article and took accountability for some missing data and explained what a pm2.5 air monitor is and how it works. It also gave a step by step of the burn permit analysis. 
* The methodology section explains:
* How the reporters collected sugar cane burn permit data and air-quality readings.
* Why 10-minute intervals were used instead of the standard 24-hour averages.
* How they adjusted the PurpleAir sensor readings using an EPA (Environmental Protection Agency) formula.
* How it is determined whether smoke was likely traveling toward Pahokee.
* Which data points were excluded because of technical problems or incomplete records.

I think this section was very effective because it allows readers to examine the reporting process and understand how the journalists reached their conclusions. 

I think the nerd box makes or breaks the story in terms of having the reader understand the data on a scientific level, so maybe having some context next to charts would have been helpful.

Etc: I loved this article. I learned about pm2.5 levels in my environmental science course last year and was very interested to learn about this topic. The scope was truly amazing. I loved the variety of experts interviewed, people who are impacted by the smoke, people who work for the factories, and even some of the legal aspects. I really liked how the article touched on all perspectives, including sugar factory workers. I also really enjoyed how this article ends with the fact that there is no solution as of now, and how that impacts the people living in the area.
One thing I would change from this story is in the methodology. I would add more sensors in the area and I would clarify some of the numerical aspects more such as the actual distance between the sensors and sugar companies. I think this kind of story needs a follow up article as well, I am curious to know more.
